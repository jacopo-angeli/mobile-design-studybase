**Title: How emotions can be used in application design**

**Exam Question:** "Explain the concept and importance of 'Emotional Design' in mobile applications. How does it leverage human psychology and the remapping of Maslow's hierarchy of needs to transform a standard user into a loyal customer?"

**Answer:**
Emotional design represents the new frontier of mobile interface development because, in a saturated market, simply being functional is no longer enough for an application to be successful. This approach exists because emotions act as a sort of "internal bookmark" or "post-it" for our memory; when an app triggers a positive emotional response, that experience is anchored in the user’s long-term memory, encouraging them to return to the app indefinitely. We can justify this importance by looking at a remapped version of **Maslow’s pyramid of needs**, where the base levels of being "functional," "reliable," and "usable" are necessary, but the ultimate peak is the **"pleasurable" layer**. From a psychological standpoint, this targets the **amygdala**, which is the oldest part of our brain and serves as the center for both emotions and memory management.

The main idea behind implementing emotional design is to treat the application as a **persona** or a "user archetype". Before development starts, a designer should create a blueprint defining the app's personality, the specific language it uses, and its **visual lexicon**, which includes the choice of fonts, icon styles, and color palettes. A common strategy within this is **humanization**, where digital objects mimic human traits to become more attractive to users. Humans have an unconscious bias toward objects that follow the **golden ratio** or those that have baby-like proportions—such as a large head relative to the body—which triggers protective and positive instincts. Developers often use **mascots** to convey this personality, drawing from a database of random messages or animations to create a sense of real-time engagement.

While these strategies offer the advantage of high user loyalty, they carry significant implications and risks regarding **context**. A developer must ensure the persona is appropriate for the service; for instance, a banking application should never have a mascot that expresses surprise or humor regarding a user's low balance. Furthermore, while informal language can humanize a technical failure, like a 404 error, it must be used carefully so the user doesn't feel the application is being "lousy" or unprofessional in a serious situation.

A concrete example of emotional design is the email service **MailChimp**, which uses a quirky monkey mascot that provides unexpected compliments and informal feedback. By transforming a technical task into an interaction with a helpful, engaging personality, they satisfy the "pleasurable" layer of the user experience and create a community of users who feel a personal connection to the brand.

**Key Concepts:**

- **Pleasurable Layer:** The peak of the remapped Maslow pyramid.
- **Amygdala:** The brain center linking emotion and long-term memory.
- **Humanization:** Mimicking human or baby-like traits to increase attraction.
- **User Archetype/Persona:** The blueprint of an app’s personality and language.
- **Visual Lexicon:** The fonts, colors, and icons that define brand identity.

**Example/Application:**
The photo-sharing site **Flickr** uses emotional design on its **404 error pages** by displaying an image of a "bad panda". While this would be inappropriate for a bank, for a creative community, it humanizes a technical failure and uses **surprise** to mitigate the user’s frustration, turning a negative moment into a memorable, pleasurable one.

**Exam Focus:**
The professor will expect you to explain that an app must go beyond being merely functional to reach the **pleasurable layer** of the hierarchy. You should be able to discuss how **emotions facilitate memory** through the amygdala and describe the components of an app **persona** (personality, language, visual lexicon). Finally, emphasize the **risk of context mismatch**, specifically why informal or humorous "human" traits are dangerous in professional or financial applications.
