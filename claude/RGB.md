**Title: The RGB Color Model**

**Exam Question:** "Describe the RGB color model. Explain its physiological basis, how it differs from a subtractive model, how color depth is defined in practice, and where RGB is used compared with YCbCr and CMYK."

**Answer:**
To understand **RGB** we should start not from the screen but from the eye, because the model is a direct consequence of human physiology rather than a mathematical convenience. The **visible spectrum** — the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum the eye can detect — spans wavelengths from roughly **380 to 750 nanometres**. The retina senses this light with two kinds of receptor: **rods**, responsible for night vision and for perceiving luminance, and **cones**, which come in exactly **three types responding respectively to red, green and blue**. Because our color perception is mediated by three receptor types, any color sensation can be reproduced by mixing three suitably chosen primaries. That is the entire justification for RGB: it has three channels because we have three kinds of cone. It is also worth noting that the eye is **more sensitive to green** than to red or blue — a fact that reappears throughout the compression chapters.

RGB is an **additive** color model. Each channel represents *emitted* light, and the color is defined by the **presence** of a combination of components: black is the absence of all light, white is all three at maximum, and mixing pairs of primaries at full intensity gives the secondaries cyan, magenta and yellow. This is why RGB is the model of **monitors, displays, projectors and any transmitted-light device** — such a device literally emits red, green and blue light and adds it together on the retina.

Its counterpart is **CMYK**, which is **subtractive**. Here the components are inks on paper, and the **absence** of a component defines the color: the ink absorbs part of the incident white light and what we perceive is the light the pigment has *not* absorbed. This is the model of **printers and printing devices**. The two models are therefore not competitors but descriptions of two physically different situations — emitted light versus reflected light — which is why converting between them is lossy at the edges and why colors on screen never match a print exactly.

A **channel**, formally, is a grayscale image of the same size as the color image, containing just one of the primary components. Decomposing a photograph into its R, G and B channels gives three grayscale images that are individually unexpressive; the perception of a particular tone only emerges from the combination of their intensities. In such a channel, black indicates the absence of that color and white its maximum presence.

The practical parameter is **color depth**, the number of bits used per pixel, and this is where the exam numbers live. **True color** uses **24 bits per pixel — 8 bits per channel**, giving 256 intensity levels per channel and $256 \times 256 \times 256 \approx$ **16.7 million** distinct colors. Variants include **48 bits** for high-definition work and **24 + 8 bits**, where the extra **alpha channel** carries per-pixel transparency or opacity. Below true color one uses **indexed color**, typically **8 bits for 256 colors** chosen from a **palette**, also called a **CLUT (Color Look-Up Table)**; at the bottom are **grayscale and monochrome** images at 8 and 1 bit. Dropping to 256 or 16 colors degrades quality visibly, and it is essential to say in an exam that **this is not compression** — it is simply a decision about how many bits are spent representing each pixel, taken before any compression algorithm runs.

Color depth is one of the two dimensions of image digitisation. Digitising an image is a **two-step process**: **spatial sampling**, which converts the continuous spatial domain into a discrete grid of pixels and thereby fixes the **resolution**; and **chromatic quantization**, which converts the continuous range of colors into a finite set of discrete values — colors represented as "bins" according to the bits allocated per channel. RGB describes the outcome of the second step.

Finally, RGB is not the only way to organise the same information, and knowing why alternatives exist is what the question is really after. **HSV** (hue, saturation, value/intensity) rearranges the geometry of the RGB cube into a hexagonal-based pyramid to be **more intuitive and perceptually relevant**: the angle selects the hue, the distance from the axis is the saturation — effectively how much white is mixed in — and the vertical dimension is intensity. HSV is used **for creating and editing images interactively**, not for representing or transmitting them, precisely because it is built around how a human chooses a color rather than how a device produces one. **YUV, YIQ and YCbCr**, by contrast, separate **luminance (Y)** from **chrominance**, and they exist for transmission and compression: Y′UV is the separation used in PAL, Y′IQ in NTSC, Y′PbPr in component video, and **Y′CbCr is the digital encoding used in JPEG and MPEG**. Historically, U and V were added to an existing luma signal on a subcarrier so that black-and-white receivers could still display a color transmission with no extra bandwidth. The reason this matters for compression is that the eye is far more sensitive to luminance detail than to chrominance detail, so the chrominance channels can be subsampled — an economy RGB cannot offer, because in RGB the brightness information is smeared across all three channels and discarding any of them damages the image visibly.

Beyond all of these sits **CIE XYZ**, a theoretical model not used in practice, created by the International Commission on Illumination to describe the **entire range of human color vision** using a **tristimulus system** that matches any color sensation against the three primaries under standardised conditions. Its chromaticity diagram — the familiar horseshoe curve of pure colors closed by the **line of purples** — is used as a reference for **calibrating monitors and televisions**.

**Key Concepts:**

- **Additive model:** Color defined by the *presence* of emitted R, G, B light. Monitors and transmitted light.
- **Subtractive model (CMYK):** Color defined by the *absence* of components; pigment absorbs, we see what is left. Printers.
- **Physiological basis:** Three cone types (red, green, blue) plus rods for luminance; the eye is most sensitive to green.
- **Channel:** A grayscale image holding one primary component.
- **True color:** 24 bits/pixel, 8 per channel, 16.7 M colors; 48 b for HD; 24 + 8 b with alpha.
- **Indexed color / CLUT:** 8 bits addressing a 256-entry palette.
- **Color depth is not compression:** It is a representation choice made before compression.
- **Two-step digitisation:** Spatial sampling (resolution) then chromatic quantization (color).
- **HSV:** RGB geometry rearranged for human intuition — used to *create* images, not represent them.
- **YCbCr:** Luminance/chrominance separation for transmission and compression (JPEG, MPEG).
- **CIE XYZ:** Theoretical tristimulus space covering all human color vision; used for calibration.

**Example/Application:**
A single photograph traverses three models on its way from camera to page. It is captured and displayed in **RGB**, because the sensor and the screen work with emitted light. To be stored as a JPEG it is first converted to **YCbCr**, so that the two chrominance channels can be subsampled and quantized far more aggressively than the luminance channel — which is the first lossy step of JPEG and the reason the conversion happens at all. To be printed in a magazine it is converted again to **CMYK**, because ink on paper is subtractive. The same image, three representations, each chosen by the physics of the device at that end of the pipeline.

**Exam Focus:**
The professor wants the **additive versus subtractive distinction stated in terms of presence versus absence of components**, tied to monitors versus printers. Give the **24-bit / 8-bit-per-channel / 16.7 million** figures and the **380–750 nm** range. Be explicit that **reducing color depth is not compression**. The strongest answers explain *why* three channels — the three cone types — rather than treating it as a convention, and close by distinguishing the three families by **purpose**: RGB to display, HSV to author, YCbCr to transmit and compress, CMYK to print. If the question pairs RGB with YCbCr, the point to make is that YCbCr separates luminance from chrominance so that chrominance can be subsampled, which RGB structurally cannot do.
